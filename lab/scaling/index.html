01 · Try it

Spend a compute budget

Nothing is trained on this page. It evaluates loss curves that the papers fit to hundreds of real training runs, so you can ask their central question: for a fixed amount of training compute, how big should the model be and how many tokens should it see? Training compute is approximated as C ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs (N parameters, D tokens).

Loss is cross-entropy in nats per token on the fits’ training distribution (DeepMind’s MassiveText, SentencePiece tokens). E is the irreducible part: the entropy of text that no model size or data budget removes in these fits.

05 · The earlier laws

Kaplan et al. (2020)

The first systematic scaling laws for transformer language models fit single-variable power laws, each holding when the other factors are not the bottleneck, plus a joint law. Their losses are in nats per token on WebText2 with GPT-2’s tokenizer, so the numbers can’t be compared directly with the Chinchilla fits above, only the shapes.

L(N) = (8.8×10¹³ / N)^0.076 · L(D) = (5.4×10¹³ / D)^0.095 · L(C) = (3.1×10⁸ PF-days / C)^0.050 · L(N, D) = [(N_c/N)^(α_N/α_D) + D_c/D]^α_D
Quantity at the budget aboveValue

Kaplan et al. concluded that compute-efficient training should grow N ∝ C^0.73, putting most extra compute into parameters. Hoffmann et al. found N ∝ C^≈0.5, scaling parameters and tokens equally. The table in 01 shows an allocation that follows the 0.73 exponent, drawn through GPT-3’s 175B parameters and 300B tokens (a model of that era), evaluated with the newer fits.

06 · Concepts

How it works

Power laws

Over many orders of magnitude, test loss falls as a power of model size, data and compute, L ∝ X−α, which is a straight line on log–log axes. The small exponents (0.05–0.37) mean each constant improvement costs a multiplicative increase in resources.

Counting compute

A forward pass costs about 2N FLOPs per token and the backward pass about twice that, so training costs C ≈ 6ND (Kaplan et al. 2020, Table 1; attention adds a term that is small for large models).

Three approaches

Hoffmann et al. estimated the optimum three ways: (1) the lower envelope of training curves, (2) IsoFLOP profiles — train many sizes at fixed compute and find the minimum, (3) fit the parametric L(N, D) above to all runs. Approaches 1 and 2 gave N ∝ C~0.5 and about 20 tokens per parameter, the rule behind Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens).

The replication

Besiroglu et al. (2024) reconstructed the Approach 3 data from the paper’s figures and refit it. They found the published fit inconsistent with Approaches 1 and 2 and its confidence intervals implausibly narrow; their refit implies roughly 20 tokens per parameter, in line with the other two approaches. Both fits are shown here.

Why Kaplan differed

Kaplan et al. used one learning-rate schedule for runs of different lengths, counted only non-embedding parameters and worked with smaller models; later analyses attribute most of the gap to such choices (e.g. Porian et al. 2024). The disagreement moved the field from GPT-3-style models (≈1.7 tokens per parameter) to Chinchilla-style training.

Beyond compute-optimal

Compute-optimal minimizes training cost for a given loss. A model that will serve many queries is cheaper overall if it is smaller and trained on far more tokens, accepting a less efficient training run (Sardana et al. 2024). That is why Llama 3 8B saw roughly 15T tokens, close to 2,000 tokens per parameter.

What the fits don’t say

They describe pretraining loss on one data distribution, not downstream ability; they assume data is unlimited and not repeated (Muennighoff et al. 2023 study repeated data); and constants change with data quality, tokenizer and architecture. Use them for shape and ratios, not exact numbers.
