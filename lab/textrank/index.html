Summarize by ranking sentences

TextRank treats every sentence as a node, connects sentences that share words, and runs PageRank: a sentence is important if it is similar to many other important sentences. The summary is the top-ranked sentences in their original order.

      02 · Concepts

      How it works

      Extractive vs. abstractive

      An extractive summarizer copies whole sentences from the source; an abstractive one writes new text. Extraction can’t fix awkward transitions or merge ideas, but it never invents facts, needs no training data, and runs instantly, which is why it remained the default for decades.

      Sentence similarity

      Mihalcea & Tarau (2004) connect sentences Si and Sj with weight

      sim = |{w : w ∈ Si ∧ w ∈ Sj}| / (log|Si| + log|Sj|)

      counting shared content words; the log terms stop long sentences from winning just by being long. LexRank (Erkan & Radev, 2004) uses TF-IDF cosine instead, which you can switch to above.

      Weighted PageRank

      WS(Vi) = (1 − d) + d · Σj [ wji / Σk wjk ] · WS(Vj)

      A random reader jumps along similarity edges with probability d and restarts at a random sentence otherwise. Iterating until scores stop changing (here, below 10⁻⁴) finds the stationary distribution. Lower d spreads scores more evenly.

      Keywords with TextRank

      The same algorithm ranks words: nodes are content words, and two words are linked if they appear within two tokens of each other. The top third of words become keywords, and adjacent keywords merge into phrases (river delta). The original paper also filters candidates by part of speech; this version uses a stopword list.

      The lead baseline

      Taking the first k sentences is a famously hard baseline for news, because journalists put the key facts first. It is weaker for speeches and essays that build to their point. Comparing both columns on the three samples shows the difference.

      Evaluation

      Summaries are usually scored with ROUGE (Lin, 2004): n-gram overlap with human-written reference summaries. ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2 count shared unigrams and bigrams; ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence. High ROUGE doesn’t guarantee a readable or faithful summary, so human evaluation is still standard.
