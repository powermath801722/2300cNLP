    03 · Concepts

    How it works

    Tokens and types

    A token is one occurrence of a word-like unit; a type is a distinct form. “the cat saw the dog” has 5 tokens and 4 types. The type/token ratio (types ÷ tokens) measures lexical variety, but it falls as texts get longer, so only compare texts of similar length.

    Why tokenizing is hard

    Whitespace leaves “dog.” and “dog” as different types. A good tokenizer separates punctuation, but must keep “3.14”, “U.S.”, “e-mail”, URLs and emoji intact, and decide whether “don’t” is one token or do + n’t (the Penn Treebank convention used here).

    Sentence segmentation

    A period ends a sentence, except after “Dr.”, “e.g.”, initials like “J. R.”, or inside “3.5”. Rule-based segmenters use abbreviation lists and context (is the next word capitalized?); Punkt (Kiss & Strunk, 2006) learns abbreviations from raw text without supervision.

    Zipf’s law

    Frequency is roughly inversely proportional to rank: f(r) ∝ 1 / rα with α ≈ 1. A few words are extremely common and most are rare: in text8, about 47% of the 254k types occur exactly once (hapax legomena).

    Heaps’ law

    Vocabulary grows sublinearly with corpus size: V(N) = K·Nβ, typically β ≈ 0.4–0.6. You never run out of new words, which is why models need strategies for unknown words: <UNK> tokens, smoothing, or subword units.

    Normalization choices

    Lowercasing, removing stopwords, stemming and Unicode normalization (NFC/NFKC) all merge types. They help count-based methods like search and topic models, and can hurt tasks where case and function words matter, such as named entity recognition or parsing.
