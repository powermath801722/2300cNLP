03 · Concepts

How it works

The HMM

Tags are hidden states, words are observations. The model assumes each tag depends only on the previous tag (transition a(i→j) = P(tⱼ | tᵢ)) and each word only on its own tag (emission b(j, w) = P(w | tⱼ)). It scores a sequence by Πₜ P(tₜ | tₜ₋₁) · P(wₜ | tₜ).

Training = counting

Both tables are relative frequencies from tagged sentences, with add-k smoothing (k = 0.1 for transitions, 0.01 for emissions) so unseen events don't get probability zero. No gradient descent is involved.

Viterbi decoding

There are |T|ⁿ possible tag sequences, but dynamic programming finds the best one in O(n·|T|²): keep only the best way to reach each tag at each position, then follow backpointers. Working in log space turns products into sums and avoids underflow.

Unknown words

Training words seen once are replaced by a signature (UNK-ing, UNK-ed, UNK-cap, UNK-num, …), so the model learns, for example, that unseen -ly words are usually adverbs. Unknown words are marked * above.

Why it fails on garden paths

“The old man the boat” needs man as a verb, but a bigram model only sees one tag back and man is overwhelmingly a noun. Fixing it needs longer context: trigram HMMs, CRFs, or neural taggers (BiLSTM, transformers reach about 97–98% on full WSJ).

Baseline

Tagging every known word with its most frequent training tag (and unknown words as NN) already gets about 89% here. Most words are unambiguous; the hard part is the remaining ambiguous tokens, where context matters.
