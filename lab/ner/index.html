02 · Concepts

How it works

NER as token classification

BERT encodes the whole sentence; a linear layer on top of each token’s final hidden state predicts one of nine labels. Because every token sees every other token through self-attention, “Washington” can be tagged a person, place or organization depending on its neighbours.

BIO (IOB2) tags

B-X begins an entity of type X, I-X continues it, O is outside any entity. Two adjacent entities of the same type stay separate because the second starts with B-. The scheme can’t express overlapping or nested entities: in “Bank of England”, England is a location inside an organization, but only one label per token is allowed.

CoNLL-2003

The training data: about 300k tokens of English Reuters newswire from 1996, annotated with four types: PER (people), ORG (organizations), LOC (locations) and MISC (nationalities, events, works and other names). Models trained on it inherit its domain: they do best on capitalized, well-edited news and degrade on lowercase chat, tweets or clinical notes.

Subwords and alignment

WordPiece splits rare words (“Lovelace” → “Love”, “##lace”). Training labels exist per word, so the usual recipe labels the first piece and ignores the rest in the loss. At prediction time we take each word’s first-piece tag and average the piece confidences, which is why a word can never be half inside an entity here.

Span-level F1

NER is scored on whole entities, not tokens: a predicted entity counts only if its boundaries and type both match the gold span exactly (the CoNLL conlleval script). Precision = correct / predicted, recall = correct / gold, F1 is their harmonic mean. Getting “Charles Babbage’s” as “Charles” alone is a miss, even though one token was right.

This model

dslim/bert-base-NER (BERT-base cased, 110M parameters) converted to ONNX and quantized to 8-bit by Xenova. Its model card reports roughly 91 F1 on the CoNLL-2003 test set. Quantization saves three quarters of the download at a small cost in accuracy.
