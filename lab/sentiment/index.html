03 · Concepts

How it works

Lexicon + rules (VADER)

Every word has a hand-rated valence from −4 to +4 (7,500 entries). Rules adjust it: a negator within three words flips and damps it (×−0.74), boosters like very add ±0.293, ALL-CAPS adds emphasis, and after but the clause counts 1.5× while the part before counts 0.5×. The sum is squashed to a compound score s / √(s² + 15) in [−1, 1].

Multinomial Naive Bayes

Pick the class maximizing log P(c) + Σ log P(wᵢ | c), assuming words are independent given the class. Probabilities are word counts per class with add-one (Laplace) smoothing. A word's contribution is log P(w|pos) − log P(w|neg). It can't see word order, so not good looks like good.

Logistic regression

A linear model on word-presence features: P(pos) = σ(b + Σ wⱼ xⱼ), trained by minimizing cross-entropy with L2 regularization (C chosen on a validation slice). Weights are learned discriminatively, so correlated words don't double-count the way they do in Naive Bayes.

Fine-tuned transformer

DistilBERT reads the whole sentence with self-attention, so each word's representation depends on its context, including negation and contrast. A classification head on the [CLS] token was fine-tuned on SST-2. It's far stronger on sentences, but its decisions are not a sum of word scores, so there's no simple per-word explanation.

Domain shift

Sentiment words are domain-dependent: an unpredictable plot is good, an unpredictable steering wheel is not. Models trained on movie reviews carry movie-review associations (e.g. names of actors, genres) into every domain they're applied to.

What all of them miss

Sarcasm, irony and implicit sentiment (“I want my two hours back”) need world knowledge and pragmatics. Even strong transformers are inconsistent on them; this is still an active research area.
