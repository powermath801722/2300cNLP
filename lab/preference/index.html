01 · Preference data

Simulated annotators with a known bias

Four prompts, six candidate answers each, written for this page. Every answer has three features: helpfulness (0–1, judged by the author), length in words, and whether it opens with flattery. Simulated annotators compare random pairs and pick a winner with a Bradley–Terry model. They mostly reward helpfulness, but like everyone, they are swayed by long and flattering answers. That bias is what the rest of the page is about. Everything here is toy-scale and exact; real RLHF uses neural reward models and sampled responses.

02 · Reward model

Fit a Bradley–Terry reward model

A linear reward r(y) = w·φ(y) on the three features, trained by minimizing −log σ(r(yw) − r(yl)) over the training pairs (20% held out). It learns what the annotators reward — including their bias.

03 · Over-optimization

Optimize the reward model, watch the gold utility

RLHF maximizes Eπ[r(y)] − β·KL(π ‖ πref). For a fixed set of answers its optimum is exact: π*(y) ∝ πref(y)·exp(r(y)/β). Lowering β lets the policy move further from the reference model. The reward model’s score keeps rising; the true (gold) utility rises, peaks, then falls as the policy exploits the length and flattery the reward model learned from annotators.

β = 1.5
04 · RLHF vs DPO

Two routes to the same objective

RLHF optimizes the policy against the trained reward model (here with exact policy gradients; real systems sample and use PPO). DPO skips the reward model: it fits the policy straight to the preference pairs with −log σ(β·[log π(yw)/πref(yw) − log π(yl)/πref(yl)]). Both use the β above.

05 · Concepts

How it works

Bradley–Terry

Each item has a latent score; P(a beats b) = σ(sa − sb) (Bradley & Terry 1952). Fitting scores to observed wins is logistic regression on score differences. A reward model is a Bradley–Terry model whose scores come from a network reading the prompt and response (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022).

KL-regularized RL

maxπ Ey∼π[r(y)] − β·KL(π ‖ πref). The KL term keeps the policy near the supervised model, both to keep text fluent and because the reward model is only trustworthy near the data it was trained on. Its optimum is the Gibbs distribution π* ∝ πref·exp(r/β).

Goodhart and over-optimization

The reward model is a proxy. Pushed hard, a policy finds what the proxy over-rewards. Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2023) measured this with a large “gold” reward model standing in for humans: gold reward rises then falls as KL from the initial policy grows, while proxy reward keeps rising. Here the gold utility is a formula, so the effect is exact.

DPO

Substitute r = β·log(π/πref) + const (the inverse of the closed-form optimum) into the Bradley–Terry likelihood and the reward model disappears: the policy is trained directly on preferences (Rafailov et al. 2023). With a tabular policy, DPO’s implicit reward follows a per-answer Bradley–Terry fit exactly, which this page checks iteration by iteration.

Why the policies differ

RLHF here uses a feature-based reward model that shares strength across all four prompts; DPO with one free logit per answer only learns from comparisons involving that answer. When one answer wins almost every comparison, its tabular score keeps growing — a known DPO failure mode with near-deterministic preferences. Both inherit the annotators’ bias.

What toy scale hides

Real policies generate text token by token, so optimization explores responses no one labelled; reward models are neural and can be fooled off-distribution; RLHF uses sampled PPO updates with variance; DPO runs on fixed offline pairs. The core trade-offs — proxy vs gold, KL budget, implicit vs explicit reward — are the same.
