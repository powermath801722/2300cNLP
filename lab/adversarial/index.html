01 · Try it

Flip a classifier by swapping words

A greedy black-box attack: rank words by how much deleting them hurts the prediction, then replace them one at a time with nearby words in the shipped Word2Vec space until the label flips. Embedding neighbours are often not synonyms, so each substitution is checked against WordNet.

02 · At scale

Attack success rate on held-out sentences

The same attack on SST-2 validation sentences of 6–20 words that the target model gets right. “Success” only means the label flipped; the WordNet columns ask whether the meaning plausibly survived. WordNet links few antonyms (it doesn’t connect worst–greatest or smart–dumb), so the antonym rate is a lower bound: read the examples.

03 · Behavioral testing

CheckList-style test suite

Capability tests in the style of Ribeiro et al. (2020): MFT minimum functionality (does the model get simple cases right?), INV invariance (a label-preserving edit shouldn’t change the prediction), DIR directional expectations (adding praise shouldn’t lower P(positive) by more than 0.1). 04 · Concepts

How it works

Threat model

Black-box, score-based: the attacker sees P(class | x) but no gradients or weights. An adversarial example is x′ with f(x′) ≠ f(x) while a human would still give x′ the original label. Text is discrete, so unlike images there are no imperceptible perturbations: every substitution is visible and may change meaning. That second condition is the hard part.

The attack

Following Jin et al. (2020, TextFooler) in simplified form: importance Ii = Py(x) − Py(x∖i) by deletion; for each important non-stopword, query every candidate substitution and keep the one that lowers Py most; if several flip the label, take the one closest in embedding space. Alzantot et al. (2018) search the same space with a genetic algorithm. Real attacks add sentence-encoder similarity and language-model fluency constraints.

Why neighbours aren’t synonyms

The distributional hypothesis puts words used in the same contexts close together, and antonyms share contexts (“the film was good/bad”). So the nearest neighbours of good include bad. An attack that “succeeds” by swapping in an antonym hasn’t fooled the model; it has changed the sentence. Counter-fitting (Mrkšić et al. 2016) pushes antonyms apart for this reason. The WordNet columns measure how often that happens here.

Behavioral testing

Held-out accuracy averages over whatever the test set happens to contain. CheckList (Ribeiro et al. 2020) borrows from software testing: list linguistic capabilities (vocabulary, negation, robustness to names, typos…) and write many targeted cases per capability. Failure rates per capability expose problems a single accuracy number hides, such as bag-of-words models ignoring negation.

Defenses and evaluation

Adversarial training, certified robustness for word substitutions (Jia et al. 2019) and constrained, human-validated attack evaluation (Morris et al. 2020, TextAttack) all start from the same point: success rates mean little unless the perturbations are shown to preserve meaning. Report both.
