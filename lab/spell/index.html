Noisy-channel spelling correction

Treat a typo as a clean word sent through a noisy keyboard. Pick the word w that maximizes P(w) · P(typo | w): how common the word is, times how likely those exact mistakes are.

03 · Concepts

How it works

The recurrence

With source x (length n) and target y (length m), let D[i, j] be the distance between the first i characters of x and the first j of y. Then D[i, 0] = i·del, D[0, j] = j·ins, and

D[i, j] = min( D[i−1, j] + del, D[i, j−1] + ins, D[i−1, j−1] + (xi = yj ? 0 : sub) )

The answer is D[n, m]. Filling the table takes O(nm) time; Wagner & Fischer (1974) gave this algorithm.

Levenshtein vs. cost choices

Levenshtein distance counts every edit as 1. Jurafsky & Martin’s textbook example uses substitution cost 2 (a substitution is a deletion plus an insertion), which makes intention → execution cost 8 instead of 5. Damerau’s variant adds swapping two adjacent letters as one edit, which matches a very common typing error (teh → the). The version here is the “optimal string alignment” form, which never edits the same substring twice.

Backtrace and alignment

Remembering which neighbor produced each cell’s minimum lets you walk back from D[n, m] to D[0, 0]. That path is an alignment: every diagonal step is a match or substitution, every vertical step a deletion, every horizontal step an insertion. Several paths can tie; this demo prefers diagonal moves.

The noisy channel

Bayes’ rule turns “which word did they mean?” into ŵ = argmaxw ∈ V P(x | w) · P(w). The prior P(w) comes from word counts in 17 million words of Wikipedia (text8). The channel model P(x | w) scores the edits: here, each substitution with a neighboring key is five times as likely as one with a distant key, and transpositions and doubled letters are cheap.

Candidate generation

Following Norvig, candidates are every dictionary word reachable by one or two deletions, transpositions, substitutions or insertions. For a word of length n there are 54n + 25 strings one edit away, and tens of thousands two edits away, but only a handful are real words. Damerau found that about 80% of misspellings are a single edit.

Real-word errors

This corrector only fixes words missing from its dictionary. “I need a peace of paper” has no unknown word, so nothing is flagged. Catching real-word errors needs context: a language model compares P(“a piece of paper”) with P(“a peace of paper”) across confusable candidates. Production systems also learn the channel from confusion matrices of real typos (Kernighan, Church & Gale, 1990).
