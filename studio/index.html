Overview

How tokenization works →

Counts come from the lab’s rule-based tokenizer and sentence splitter. Readability uses the Flesch formulas: shorter sentences and shorter words read more easily. Lexical diversity is the moving-average type–token ratio over 50-word windows, so long and short texts compare fairly.

Sentiment

Compare the methods →

Three models score the text. The lexicon-and-rules model (VADER) is averaged over sentences; Naive Bayes and logistic regression, trained on movie reviews, read the whole text as a bag of words. When they disagree, the text is usually mixed, sarcastic or far from movie reviews. The arc shows how the tone moves sentence by sentence.

Grammar & style

Tagging → · Spelling →

A hidden Markov model tags every word with its part of speech (95% accurate on held-out newswire). From the tags: the mix of nouns, verbs and modifiers, likely passive constructions (a form of “be” or “get” followed by a past participle), and sentence lengths. Spelling flags words missing from a 60,000-word dictionary and ranks corrections with a noisy-channel model; rare names and technical terms are often flagged too, so treat these as suggestions.

Key ideas

How TextRank works →

TextRank builds a graph of sentences that share content words and runs PageRank on it: the sentences most connected to the rest of the text make the summary, shown in their original order. Keywords come from the same algorithm run over words.

How NER works →

A BERT model fine-tuned on the CoNLL-2003 newswire benchmark finds people, organizations, locations and other names (MISC: nationalities, events, works). It downloads once (≈109 MB) and then runs locally.

AI-text signals

Surprisal and GLTR →
These signals are not proof of who or what wrote a text. They measure how predictable each word is to a small language model (distilgpt2). Formulaic, edited, translated or non-native writing can look “predictable”, and lightly edited machine text can look human. Never use them alone to accuse anyone.

For each token: surprisal (how many bits of information it carried) and its rank in the model’s predictions, colored as in GLTR (Gehrmann, Strobelt & Rush, 2019). Machine-generated text tends to have a higher share of top-10 tokens and lower perplexity than human writing. The first 600 tokens are analyzed.
